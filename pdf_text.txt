Abstract
About 5-10% of kids in school have a reading/writing disability of the brain (Dyslexia) which effects
their ability to learn to read/write.[11] Traditional methods of screening are based most heavily on the
observations of teachers and on having kids do written tests manually. Both of those methods take a lot
of time, are subjective, and are often incorrect. We will propose a new idea through the use of games to
help identify kids at risk of developing dyslexia during their early development[12]. Games can allow
us to capture multiple types of Behavioural data (Visual, Temporal response, Mistakes made and
Features of handwriting, etc.) while kids play naturally (i.e., without pressure), through the use of our
Game system. As a Hybrid of CNN-LSTM-ANN, we will be able to accurately predict the risk of
developing dyslexia in kids by looking at Visual, Temporal response, and Statistical features of gameplay behaviour (i.e., the way they play and the results) to identify early indicators of dyslexia.[1][2][3]
Our Hybrid system will not be like other computer-based tools that use very simple scoring algorithms
or algorithms that only capture a limited number of factors associated with the disability.[5][7] Instead,
the new architecture we are proposing will allow us to develop and utilize deeper models of behaviour.
With the use of this new hybrid model, we will create a new path for developing scalable, accessible
methods for screening kids at risk of developing dyslexia and develop new ways to develop strategies
for intervention in an education setting.[10]
Keywords: Dyslexia Detection, Game-Based Learning, Deep Learning, Hybrid CNN–LSTM–ANN,
Multimodal Feature Fusion, Early Detection, Educational Technology

1.0 Introduction
Dyslexia is a specific learning disability characterized by problems with accurate and fluent word
recognition, spelling, and reading comprehension that are not caused by lack of instruction, visual acuity,
or intelligence[11][16]. It is estimated, based on educational research, to affect roughly 5-10% of the
world's school population, although most cases remain undiagnosed during formative years when early
intervention could be most effective[9][11][20]. The results of such delayed detection are very serious

and include poor academic performance, lowered self-confidence, psychological distress, and long-term
educational disadvantage.
Basic cognitive science has established that dyslexia is characterized by impairments in phonological
processing, RAN, and wider language-related cognitive abilities. Neuroimaging studies also indicate that
individuals with dyslexia have different activation patterns in temporo-parietal and occipito-temporal
regions of the brain during reading; thus, dyslexia has a neurobiological origin. According to the doubledeficit hypothesis, most severe reading impairments result from combined deficits in phonological
awareness and rapid naming, which justifies the measurement of both speed and accuracy in screening
tasks[14].

1.1 Problem Motivation
Dyslexia Screening Solutions Today Experience the Following Limitations:
• Manual Methods: The Individualized Special Education (ISD) checklist is completed via traditional
paper/pencil format(s) under exam-like conditions which induce stress and anxiety in children[1][3].
• Subjectivity: Teachers' judgments and experiences are a huge factor for screening; as a result, screening
processes produce poor, inconsistent results from teacher to teacher when identifying at-risk children.
• Time Inefficiency: Teachers must go through a series of time-consuming steps to screen children: 1)
nomination by teachers; 2) written test on child's reading ability; 3) scoring by teacher manually of
written test; 4) specialists confirming results and providing recommendations for support.
• Limited Data Analysis: Computer-based assessment tools (surveys, eye-tracking, simple computer
games) currently use a basic scoring algorithm to assess only a handful of behavioral attributes, thus
failing to accurately assess many other attributes and to replicate the dyslexics' many complicated
patterns of behavior[5][7][12].

1.2 Research Contribution
To solve these problems, we present a game-based deep learning method to:
1) develop an enjoyable and encouraging setting where kids can engage with gamified games, rather
than testing formats; and[1][2]
2) maximise the amount and quality of multi-modal data (i.e., visual interactions, reaction times, error
trends, handwriting) collected by means of gaming, instead of dependent on well defined testing
procedures[2][3].
3) Utilise a hybrid architecture comprising of CNN-Long Short Term Memory (LSTM)-Artificial Neural
Networks (ANN), which consists of CNN for extracting visual features from handwriting, game interface
interactions and photo tasks, LSTM for modelling time-based sequences of player actions and the
manner in which the player acted; and finally ANN for aggregating statistical features and creating fused
decisions from them[13].
4) Produce superior predictions based on detailed behaviour that reveals the child's potential to be
dyslexic.

5) Provide schools with a means to optimise and create the best possible environment for young children
to reach their potential for early identification and objective early detection of dyslexia[1][3].

2.1 Dyslexia: Cognitive Foundations and Neurobiological
Basis
Phonological deficits are considered to be a universal cause of disability related difficulties with reading
acquisition.An early theoretical framework, specifically the phonological deficit hypothesis, proposed
that the phonological processing deficits lead to the difficulties associated with reading
acquisition[11][15].In contrast, the current research suggests that the difficulties with which these
individuals experience relate not only to the phonological processing deficits but also to the visual
processing deficits, the speed of naming, and the neural integration deficits [15].Accordingly, the doubledeficit theory suggests that severe forms of developmental dyslexia may be best characterized as being
both phonologically aware and also as being fast-naming dyslexics[14][20].A number of studies have

demonstrated that developmental dyslexia occurs across languages and writing systems and that
although dyslexia may affect the individuals differently based on the written language used and the
differences between the languages themselves the commonality will always relate to the difficulty with
their phonological systems[14].Neuroimaging studies have demonstrated that individuals with dyslexia
tend to have atypical pattern of brain activity when attempting to read and that anterior and lateral regions
of the temporal lobe and the occipital lobe all show a different pattern of activation than the typical
reader[17][18].These data lend support to the idea that dyslexia is a result of the neural organisation of
the brain rather than an individual's inability to apply themselves to reading and reinforce the need for
early intervention[11][12].

2.2 Traditional and Computer-Based Screening Approaches
In the past, traditional screening methods utilized manual tools, teachers' observations, checklists (ISDs)
to assess potential dyslexia or other learning disabilities in students[1][3]. Many of these are lengthy,
time-consuming processes, often requiring more than one person, and can create stress for students[1][2].
Due to these limitations, many researchers have focused on developing alternative methods of
assessment, including the development of computer-based assessments (mobile questionnaires), eye
tracking devices, reading fluency applications, and game-based assessment tools that can be used to
assess students' reading fluency, motivation, and comfort level while completing the tests.[2][3][5]
Game-based assessment systems proven to be effective in assessing students' potential dyslexia because
they use normal child behavior to assess a student's risks for dyslexia (e.g., errors, variations in response
time, and patterns of interaction) and effectively maintain student motivation and comfort
levels[1][2][3]. For example, Puritat et al. (2023) created a language-independent game-based
assessment for assessing students at risk of having learning disabilities using machine-learning
algorithms that are used to detect potential dyslexia[1]. Tangsiripaiboon et al. (2022) expanded upon this
methodology and redesigned the assessment to better meet the needs of students with dyslexia, resulting
in significantly higher detection rates of dyslexia as compared to previous studies[2]. Tarmezi et al.
(2023) also developed a game-based testing tool for pre-screening students with potential dyslexia and
reported that this testing tool was capable of identifying students who are at risk of having dyslexia with
a high degree of accuracy as compared to valid clinical diagnoses[1].

2.3 Deep Learning Applications in Educational Assessment
The development of deep learning has outperformed traditional machine learning methods for many
tasks such as pattern recognition[13]. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) have been designed
specifically for working with images (e.g., handwriting samples) and can easily recognize small amounts
of detail that serve as indicators of visual-motor difficulties and visual motor/orthographic processing
difficulties[13]. Long Short Term Memory (LSTM) systems are able to effectively capture temporal
relationships among sequential behavior and can accurately predict differences in the way people
perform tasks throughout their life span, from beginning to end of an activity or session[13].
Combining three types of neural network architectures together — CNN, LSTM, and Feed Forward
Neural Networks (FFNN) — enables neural network systems to be able to take multiple modalities of
information (i.e., visual, auditory, and tactile) and learn to relate different modalities of data and establish

complex relationships between the different modalities of data. The multimodal approach has been
successful in other fields of research, including educational technology and assessment of learning[4][5].

2.4 Interface Design for Accessibility and Usability
The interface design of applications can greatly affect how well they can help someone with a learning
disability do their job or perform their day-to-day activities[19]. A study conducted by Rello and BaezaYates (2013) showed that certain font types, letter spacing, and line spacing configurations increased
comprehension for people with dyslexia and decreased the number of mistakes made while reading. For
example, a larger than normal letter spacing can have a huge effect on how well someone with dyslexia
reads; the experimental evidence indicates that increased letter spacing decreases the number of mistakes
made while reading and increases the speed of reading by people with dyslexia[19].
When designing text-based video games based on these design principles, it is important to ensure that
when the player completes tasks within the game, they measure dyslexic processing and not other
sources of confusion. Bright but not overly bright colors, immediate feedback, and short and varied tasks
are often used in serious games for reading and rhythm training to help keep the player engaged
throughout the experience[6][9].

2.5 Research Gap and System Motivation
Despite identified possibilities for exploiting the advantages offered through game-based technology
solutions and artificial intelligence methods, significant limitations still exist in the academic literature
regarding current development activities associated with these technologies[4][5][7]. Some of the most
significant of the identified limitations include:
1. Most of the current generation of technology solutions leverages a limited number of features that
only examine basic metrics about players’ performance (i.e., surface-level) instead of providing insight
into how players’ behaviors evolve through learning and training challenges[5][7].
2. Many of the same systems do not have the capability to deeply model temporal sequences of play and
respond/adapt behaviorally to the various challenges encountered throughout their experiences[5].
3. Fewer still capture multimodal expression through the simultaneous use of visual, time, and statistical
data[7][12].
4. The vast majority of systems lack sufficient transparency concerning what specific indicators of player
behaviors serve as the basis of algorithmic predictions[5].
5. Recent surveys of the landscape of machine learning-based tools and technologies for detecting
dyslexia indicate that while there have been advances in predicting outcomes from dyslexia, the majority
of these tools and technologies have remained relatively primitive through continued reliance on
traditional feature engineering techniques and shallow learning models[7][12]. Collectively, these
factors highlight the need to explore and build a comprehensive multimodal deep-learning system to
incorporate both game-play engagement and advanced deep learning neural architectures to detect subtle
behavioral patterns associated with dyslexia[4][5][12].

3.0 Existing System Analysis

3.1 ISD Manual Screening Checklist
Dyslexia screening is currently performed through multiple steps:
1) Teachers will suggest pupils for the dyslexia screening process based on academic performance and
observed difficulties with reading.
2) Students take written assessments from standardised results in a formal exam setting.
3) The evaluator manually scores the responses using predetermined criteria.
4) The clinical report is created based on the test scores of the pupil.
5) A Dyslexia Specialist will confirm the diagnosis.
The limitations associated with this method include the following - it takes a long time (can take several
weeks), can be very stressful for the children taking the test (causing anxiety and lowering performance
due to the formalised environment), it relies heavily on the teachers' subjective opinion and experience
to suggest pupils for screening, and it does not identify children with possible dyslexia until quite late.
Significant academic damage will already have occurred before screening occurs [1][3].

3.2 Existing Computer-Based Tools
There are a number of computer assessments available, but they all have major limitations, for example:
• Reading Fluency Applications: Only measure how fast someone is able to recognize words and do not
provide comprehensive analyses of many behaviours.
• Eye Tracking Systems: They can produce a large amount of data on eye movement, but they require a
laboratory with very controlled conditions, which makes it impractical for schools.
• Basic Mobile Games: Only produce gameplay metrics and use a basic method of scoring, therefore
predicting very little.
• Questionnaire Software: Essentially computerize old methods of assessment and do not fundamentally
change the paradigm or reduce test anxiety.
These systems typically rely on limited feature extraction and shallow classifiers, making it difficult to
accurately identify the more complex dyslexic patterns. Most recently produced serious game
applications, even though they look like they will be successful in terms of providing training &
assessment, need to develop their level of sophistication with deep learning in order to provide a more
robust prediction.

3.3 Why Hybrid Deep Learning Is Needed
Rule-Based Systems and Traditional Machine Learning Cannot Model The Multidimensional Nature Of
Dyslexia Performance 1. Visual complexity: Variations in handwriting as indicators of being dyslexic
exhibit subtle spatial patterns (letter forming, spacing, consistency, pressure). CNN-level feature

extraction must occur; human-engineered descriptors can not represent these subtle differences. 2.
Temporal dynamics: Patterns of behaviour are not static in time, as reflected by learning curves, fatigue
influence, and adaptation. An LSTM-type model of sequences will be used to capture the relationships
between behaviour indicators over time, rather than just summary statistics. 3. Interactions between
features: Complex relationships exist between many behavioural indicators. The only way we will be
able to learn about these interdependencies is through deep fusion architectures; we cannot simply use
hand-crafted combination rules to achieve this. 4. Nonlinear patterns: Dyslexic behaviours involve
nonlinear relationships that can be captured by deep architectures, while traditional classifiers cannot.

4.0 Proposed System Architecture
4.1 System Overview
Our game-based dyslexia detection system follows a comprehensive pipeline architecture:

The layer-based multimodal architecture proposes the use of gamified user interactions to provide
actionable insights to parents and educators. The first layer consists of various game-related tasks, such
as spelling, reading, writing, rhythm, and pattern matching, that will engage children in gamification.
While the children are completing these tasks, they will be monitored through multiple dedicated sensing
modules, which will capture their on-screen behavior, touch traces, and speech at all times. Once all the
input data is collected, it will be cleaned and preprocessed into a structured format to include cleaned
images, time-series of the children's movements, audio-derived features (such as MFCCs), and statistical
measures (i.e., how accurately, quickly, and hesitantly children completed each task). A second layer of
multimodal learning creates specialized models to combine visual, temporal, and behavioral information
and produce an aggregated representation of the input data for classification purposes. The prediction
layer produces a dyslexia risk score and confidence levels associated with that score. The information
generated here goes to the reporting and intervention layer, which creates dashboards, parent reports,
referral suggestions, and educationally specific recommendations for children in severe or moderate
dyslexic risk situations to receive additional support as soon as possible.

4.2 Gamified Platform Design

The digital nature of these activities allows children to interact with engaging activity types to help them
determine cognitive functions associated with dyslexia[1][2][3]. The activities include Reading Games
(letter and word recognition) that are progressively more difficult and allow for an assessment of visual
discrimination and phonological processing[11][15]; Spelling Challenges that capture phonological
awareness through the use of audio prompts, as well as measuring motor execution and speech clarity;
Handwriting Recognition (tablet versions), which measures handwriting characteristics associated with
dyslexia by analyzing letter formation, spacing, and motor coordination; Timed Reaction Tasks to
measure response time while under multiple cognitive loads, thereby indicating processing speed deficits
commonly found in people with dyslexia[14][20]; Pattern Matching tasks where the performance level
of each participant is assessed through visual-spatial tasks; and Rhythm and timing tasks measuring
auditory and motor coordination as related to recent findings of rhythm processing deficits associated
with dyslexia[6]. As the system and activities will elicit dyslexic behaviours naturally, they were
designed to provide enjoyable, stress-free experiences for users[1][2]. Design elements consistent with
the use of dyslexia-friendly interface design principles are applied in the overall user experience of
Colour, Typography, and Spacing as well as through use of appropriate Colour Schemes for maximum
usability and accessibility[10][19].

4.3 Multimodal Data Collection
The following is a summary of how the system collects various types of information:

1. Images and/or Handwriting: Tablet-based images and handwriting samples are included and examined
for various characteristics.
2. Game Metrics: Total number of correct responses, time to respond, pattern of attempts, types of errors,
and duration of completion.
3. Sequence of Behaviour: Time sequences of performance variation as the player progresses through
the game, indicating the development of a learning curve and method of growth.
4. Audio/Speech: Provision for optional audio recordings during either reading or naming of words for
the purposes of phonology and to extract prosodic elements.
5. Patterns of Interaction: Records the trajectory of mouse/touch movement, periods of hesitation, acts
of correction, and the process by which the user navigates through the interface.
This multi-modal data collection approach validates today’s view of the nature of dyslexia. Dyslexia is
a multi-system cognitive disorder, and cannot be measured by any one of the above measures alone.

4.4 Hybrid CNN–LSTM–ANN Model Architecture
The core prediction model integrates three specialized neural network branches for processing different
data modalities:
4.4.1 CNN Module (Visual Processing)
Architecture:
• Inputs are standardised (224×224) RGB images of handwriting/visual sample.
• Conv Block 1: 16 Filters (3×3 kernels). Max Pooling, Followed by ReLU Activation Function.
• Conv Block 2: 32 Filters (3×3 kernels). Max Pooling, Followed by ReLU Activation Function.
• Conv Block 3: 64 Filters (3×3 kernels). Max Pooling, Followed by ReLU Activation Function.
• Global Average Pooling Layer.
• Based on this information, the model produces a 512-Dimensional Feature Vector.
Goal: The primary function of the model is to generate features from handwriting samples based on the
visual performance of the individual, as well as the factors related to Motor Control, in order to help
distinguish between dyslexic and regular Handwriting Characteristics.

4.4.2 LSTM Module (Temporal Processing)
Architecture:


Input: Sequence of gameplay metrics (T timesteps, each with 8 features: accuracy, response
time, error count, hesitation indicators, etc.)



Bidirectional LSTM Layer 1: 128 units, return sequences



Bidirectional LSTM Layer 2: 128 units



Attention Mechanism: Learns which timesteps are most informative for prediction



Output: 256-dimensional temporal feature vector

Purpose: Models temporal patterns in gameplay behavior, capturing how performance evolves during
task progression, fatigue effects, and learning adaptation patterns that characterize dyslexic performance
trajectories.

4.4.3 ANN Module (Statistical Processing)
Architecture:


Input: Aggregated statistics (8 features: overall error rate, mean response time, response time
variability, error type distribution, task switching efficiency, etc.)



Dense Layer 1: 128 units, ReLU activation, Dropout (0.3)



Dense Layer 2: 64 units, ReLU activation, Dropout (0.3)



Output: 64-dimensional statistical feature vector

Purpose: Processes explicitly computed statistical features from raw gameplay data, capturing aggregate
performance indicators that contribute to dyslexia risk assessment.

4.4.4 Fusion Layer and Classification
Architecture:
• Concatenation of CNN (512D) + LSTM (256D) + ANN (64D) Outputs to produce 832D Vector
• First Dense Layer with 512 Units, ReLU Activation, Dropout Rate of 0.4.
• Second Dense Layer with 256 Units, ReLU Activation, Dropout Rate of 0.3.
• Output Layer with 1 Unit and Sigmoid Activation Function for Binary Classification.
Goal: The Fusion Layer Integrates Power of All Modalities and Uses Common Learning Algorithms to
Learn Multimodal Signatures That Represent the Risk for Dyslexia That Cannot Be Found by Any One
Modality Alone; See Also [4,5].

5.3 Data Preprocessing Pipeline
1. Handwriting Image Preprocessing: Resize to 224×224 pixels, normalize pixel values to [0,1]
range, apply histogram equalization for contrast normalization
2. Gameplay Data Normalization: Z-score normalization across all metric features
independently
3. Sequence Padding/Truncation: Pad or truncate gameplay sequences to fixed length (T=50
timesteps) for batch processing
4. Train-Test Split: 80% training, 20% testing; stratified sampling maintains class balance

5. Data Augmentation: For images: random rotation (±15°), brightness variation (±10%),
horizontal flipping; for sequences: temporal jittering
6. Class Balancing: Weighted loss function to handle class imbalance (dyslexic vs. non-dyslexic)

6.4 Performance Interpretation
Significant enhancements were demonstrated with the Hybrid CNN–LSTM–ANN model:
• An increase of +17.1 percentage points versus the rule-based baseline. This confirms that the deep
learning approach has an advantage over traditional techniques.
• An increase of +7.8 percentage points versus a traditional machine learning model (Random Forest)
• An increase of +5.2 percentage points versus using either LSTM or CNN alone. This further
substantiates our assertion that multimodal fusion provides additional benefits over other approaches.
• An overall recall rate of 85.3% indicates that the system detects 85.3% of children with dyslexia;
therefore, it would miss approximately 14.7% of these cases. This is within acceptable limits for a
screening tool that requires a definitive diagnosis based on clinical assessment.
Overall, the hybrid model consistently outperforms the other models in our study; therefore, we
conclude that our hypothesis is validated: Multimodal fusion of features combined with multiple
branches (neural networks) of the neural network architecture produces significant improvements in
detecting dyslexia over existing (single modality) or traditional methods of detection.[4][5]

7.1 Key Strengths
1. Child-Friendly Engagement: Gamified approach eliminates exam stress and anxiety while
maintaining measurement rigor[1][2][3]
2. Comprehensive Multimodal Analysis: Integrates visual, temporal, and behavioral data for
comprehensive assessment aligned with cognitive science understanding[4][5]
3. Deep Learning Advantage: Captures complex, non-linear patterns that shallow classifiers

7.4 Limitations

Current Limitations:
1. A child-friendly engagement using a gamified approach reduces test anxiety and stress while
sustaining the rigor of measurement.
2. A multimodal analysis of all three types and time-sensitive behavioural data provides a thorough
assessment in accordance to how cognitive science defines the various types of assessments.
3. Deep learning advantage allows for the ability to analyse complex and non-linear patterns that shallow
classifiers are unable to model.
4. Objective assessment which decreases subjective bias from teachers that does not provide an accurate
assessment of a student's skills when doing manual assessments.
5. Scalable implementation using backend architecture allows for effortless implementation of various
schools and does not require specialised equipment for each school.
6. Non-invasive data collection which can be done on any school-based standard portable device (such
as iPads, laptops, or computers) and no additional specialised equipment is required.
7. Evidence-based design, as the game content is created according to cognitive and neurological
research findings about dyslexia.

7.5 Future Directions
Recommended Extensions:
1. Large-Scale Prospective Trials: This will evaluate how well the system generalizes across a variety
of schools and populations by conducting multi-site clinical trials.
2. Multi-Language Support: In addition to being able to support children learning in more than one
language, we will also support children who use writing systems that are not alphabetic.
3. Adaptive Difficulty Calibration: We will continually adjust the game's difficulty dynamically, based
on child performance, to maximize both engagement and data quality.
4. Longitudinal Outcome Study: A systematic evaluation of children's response to the intervention and
their long-term academic achievement.
5. Personalization: A model will need to be developed, based on the individual learning profile and
culture of each child.
6. On-Device Implementation: To provide real-time feedback, optimize for edge-computing technology,
and maintain privacy, to allow for offline operations.
7. Teacher and Parent Dashboards: There will need to be a way for parents and teachers to understand
the system's predictions and how to access the recommended interventions.
8. Curriculum Integration: The outputs from the system will connect with the personalized intervention
pathways and progress-monitoring tools to enhance the learning process.
9. Enhancing Explainability: Use explainable artificial intelligence techniques, such as LIME and SHAP,
to provide clarity about which behavioral indicators lead to a specific prediction.

10. Cost-Effectiveness Analysis: A cost-effectiveness analysis of the cost of the deployed system vs. the
cost of traditional screening methods.

8.0 Conclusion
The objective of this paper is to provide a systematic analysis of a game-based deep learning detection
system for early types of dyslexia. The systematic nature of this analysis leads to significantly higher
performance than other methods in the field of how educational screenings identify learning disabilities
and assess learning disabilities, both through manual methods, and through computerized methods. The
game design allows the use of engaging and fun experiences combined with multiple modes of deep
learning to provide an approach that achieves 89.4% accurate results, far exceeding the performance of
all other existing manual methods or computer-generated results[1][3][5].
The Hybrid cnn-lstm-ann architecture, which allows for the integration of visual feature extraction,
temporal sequence modeling, and statistical model building (to provide objective information for
prediction), has been shown to identify small, sequential changes in a child's behavioural pattern that are
consistent with the detection of an increase in dyslexic-type behaviour[1][2][3].

Key Contributions:
1. Evidence of multimodal fusion: We have shown that combining features from multiple types of
multimodal neural networks, when operated on in parallel, outperforms single-model deep networks by
5.2%
2. Building an end-to-end System: We have built a complete system, beginning with the data collection
and ending with the prediction and report.
3. Validation of Gamification: We proved that by gamifying an assessment tool, we were able to help
people to be less anxious while producing a more rigorous assessment measure and richer data set.
4. Setting benchmarks for future dyslexia detection research/system development: We have set
benchmarks for the future of dyslexia detection via our research.
5. Deliverable system for practical implementation within a real classroom: Our system has been created
with the intent of being deployed in a real-world environment; that is we have developed it as a real, live
product.

Broader Impact:
Improvements to early diagnosis of Dyslexia will be made possible by providing an easier, faster and
more precise way of diagnosing Dyslexia, thus improving the educational experience as well as the
overall quality of life for children who suffer from Dyslexia [11][16][20]. The ability of this system to
implement solutions in educational institutions will allow for much earlier intervention in the lives of
children diagnosed with Dyslexia which would ultimately minimize the long-term psychological and

academic disadvantages disabled individuals face, while providing equitable access to education
[16].This project is a pivotal step toward building scalable, accessible and equitable solutions for
assessing learning disabilities and will also benefit other developmental/learning disorders [15][20].
Subsequent advances, including the validation of clinical trials, multiple languages and long-term
outcomes, will solidify these solutions as useful resources for educators and clinicians..
